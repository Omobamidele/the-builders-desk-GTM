# Chapter 1 — The Cold Email Mindset

*"Before you learn how to write a cold email, you need to understand
what a cold email is actually supposed to do. If you get this wrong,
everything else you learn today becomes much harder."*

Let me start with a quick question.

Be honest with yourself here.

How many of you have ever sent a cold email that got ignored?

Most people usually say yes.

Now here's the second question.

What was your first thought when it got ignored?

Was it something like:

"I need a better template."

"I need better copy."

"I need more personalization."

"I need a stronger subject line."

If you've ever thought that, you're not alone.

Almost everyone starts there.

But here's the problem.

Most of the time, the email isn't the real issue.

The real issue is how you're thinking about the email before you even
write it.

And that's what we're going to fix first.

Because once your mindset changes, your writing naturally follows.

### What Is a Cold Email?

Let's strip this down to the simplest possible definition.

A cold email is an email sent to someone who doesn't know you.

That's it.

Nothing fancy. Nothing complicated.

But here's where most people go wrong.

They start adding assumptions to that definition.

They think a cold email is:

- A pitch
- A sales message
- A chance to explain their business
- A way to "convince" someone

None of that is true.

A cold email is simply the first interaction between two people.

That's all.

Now let me ask you something.

Think about the last time you met someone new in real life.

Did you walk up to them and immediately start selling something?

Did you list your achievements for ten minutes straight?

Did you ask them to commit to something big right away?

Of course not.

You started a conversation.

You asked questions.

You looked for common ground.

You tried to understand them first.

Cold email is exactly the same thing.

The only difference is the medium.

Instead of speaking face to face, you're doing it through email.

But the psychology is identical.

### The Biggest Mistake in Cold Email

Let's clear up one of the biggest misconceptions right now.

Most people think the goal of a cold email is to get a sale.

It isn't.

Let me say that again because it changes everything.

The goal of a cold email is not to make a sale.

The goal is to earn a reply.

That's it.

Now pause for a second and think about that.

If someone replies with:

"Yes, tell me more."

"Not right now."

"We already use someone else."

Even a "no" is still a win.

Why?

Because a reply means a conversation has started.

And conversations are where opportunities are created.

Let me give you a simple analogy.

Imagine you meet someone for the first time and say:

"Hi, I'm David. Will you marry me?"

It sounds ridiculous, right?

Why?

Because you skipped every step in between.

You didn't build trust.

You didn't build familiarity.

You didn't earn anything.

Yet that's exactly what most cold emails do.

They jump straight to:

"Book a call with me."

"Let's do a demo."

"Here's my proposal."

They ask for commitment before earning attention.

Cold email doesn't work like that.

It works in stages.

First, you earn a reply.

Then a conversation.

Then a meeting.

Then a proposal.

Then a client.

Each step unlocks the next.

### Your Email Is Not Competing With Other Emails

Now let's shift your thinking again.

Most people believe their email is competing with other sales emails.

That's not true.

Your email is competing with everything in a person's life.

Think about your own inbox for a second.

What do you usually see there?

- Client messages
- Invoices
- Meeting invites
- Internal updates
- Newsletters
- Receipts
- Reminders
- Support tickets
- Personal messages

Now imagine a stranger shows up in that inbox asking for your attention.

Do you think you're going to carefully read every word?

Probably not.

You'll make a decision in seconds.

Sometimes even faster.

That means your email isn't competing with another agency.

It isn't competing with another freelancer.

It's competing with everything else happening in that person's day.

This is why most cold emails fail.

Not because they are "badly written."

But because they don't earn attention fast enough.

Your job is not to force someone to read your email.

Your job is to make it feel worth reading.

### Stop Thinking Like the Sender

Let me show you something.

I'm going to read you two email openings.

Tell me which one feels more natural.

#### Email One

Hi

My name is Daniel

I'm the founder of Growth Masters Agency

We've helped businesses increase revenue using innovative marketing
strategies for over five years

Now pause there.

Let me ask you.

Who is this email about?

It's about Daniel.

Everything in that opening is focused on him.

Now let's look at another one.

#### Email Two

Hi Sarah

I came across your recent interview where you spoke about expanding your
customer success team, and one thing you said stood out to me

Now let me ask you the same question.

Who is this email about?

It's about Sarah.

Before we've even introduced ourselves, we've entered her world.

And that is the key difference.

People don't care about you first.

They care about themselves first.

That's not selfish.

That's human nature.

If your email immediately feels relevant to their world, you've already
done something powerful.

You've earned attention.

### The Psychology of Attention

Let's end this chapter with something important.

Every successful cold email has one thing in common.

It earns attention before it asks for action.

Think about walking through a busy airport.

People are talking.

Announcements are playing.

Phones are ringing.

Everyone is distracted.

Now imagine someone suddenly says your name.

You turn immediately.

Why?

Because your brain is constantly filtering information.

It ignores almost everything.

But the moment something feels personally relevant, it stops.

Cold email works exactly the same way.

Your prospect's inbox is that airport.

Most emails get ignored instantly.

The ones that succeed are the ones that make the reader think:

"This might be relevant to me."

And that is where everything in this course begins.

### Why Do People Reply to Cold Emails?

Now that we've changed the way we think about cold emails, let's answer
another important question.

**Why do people reply at all?**

Think about your own inbox.

When you receive an email from someone you don't know, what makes you
decide to reply?

It usually isn't because they have the best product.

It isn't because they have the fanciest website.

It isn't because they called themselves an award-winning agency.

You reply because something about the email makes you feel it's worth
responding to.

That feeling usually comes from one or more psychological triggers.
Understanding these triggers will make you a better cold email writer
because you'll stop relying on guesswork and start writing with
intention.

Let's look at a few of the most important ones.

#### Relevance

We've already touched on this, but it's so important that it deserves to
be repeated.

People don't pay attention to everything.

They pay attention to what feels relevant to them.

Imagine you're buying your first car.

Suddenly, you start noticing that same model everywhere on the road.

Were those cars always there?

Of course they were.

Your brain simply decided they were now relevant.

Your prospect's inbox works exactly the same way.

If your email doesn't immediately connect with something they care
about, their brain filters it out.

This is why relevance will become one of the biggest themes throughout
this course.

#### Curiosity

Curiosity is powerful because it creates an open loop.

When we notice something unfinished, our brains naturally want to close
that gap.

This doesn't mean writing clickbait subject lines or making exaggerated
claims.

Instead of saying,

"I found three problems on your website."

You might say,

"While looking through your website, I noticed something that made me
curious."

The second approach invites a conversation rather than making an
accusation.

Remember, curiosity should open a door, not create false expectations.

#### Credibility

Imagine two strangers email you.

One says,

"We're the best marketing agency in the world."

The other says,

"We've recently helped three companies in your industry improve their
onboarding process."

Who would you trust more?

Probably the second.

Why?

Because credibility comes from evidence, not adjectives.

One of the biggest mistakes beginners make is trying to sound impressive
instead of sounding believable.

There's a difference.

#### Simplicity

Have you ever opened an email and been greeted by one giant block of
text?

What did you do?

Most people close it immediately.

Not because they couldn't read it.

Because it looked like work.

People don't read emails the way they read books.

They scan.

Your email should feel easy to consume.

Short paragraphs.

Simple words.

One clear idea.

The easier it is to process, the more likely it is to receive a reply.

#### Low Commitment

This is one of the most overlooked principles in cold outreach.

Every request carries a level of commitment.

Think about these two questions.

"Would you be available for a 45-minute product demonstration next
Tuesday?"

Or...

"Would you be open to hearing a few ideas?"

Which feels easier to say yes to?

The second one.

Not because it's a better offer.

Because it requires less commitment.

People naturally resist large commitments from strangers.

They are much more willing to take small steps.

That's why your call to action should match the stage of the
relationship.

You're not asking someone to become your client.

You're asking them to continue the conversation.

#### The Principle That Ties Everything Together

If there's one idea I want you to leave this chapter with, it's this:

**People don't reply because your email is clever. They reply because
your email feels relevant, credible, easy to read, and easy to respond
to.**

Everything else we'll learn in this masterclass, research,
personalization, structure, subject lines, follow-ups, and lead magnets
builds on those four principles.

# Chapter 2 — Research, Relevance and Understanding Your Prospect

*"Most people think writing is the hardest part of cold emailing. It
isn't. Research is. If you don't know who you're writing to, no template
in the world will save you."*

Now that we've changed the way we think about cold emails, let's talk
about what separates average cold emails from exceptional ones.

Research.

If I asked everyone in this room how long they spend writing a cold
email, most people would probably say somewhere between fifteen and
thirty minutes.

Now let me ask another question.

How long do you spend researching the person before you write that
email?

For many people, the answer is less than two minutes.

Some don't research at all.

And that's exactly why their emails sound generic.

Here's a rule I want you to remember throughout your outbound journey.

**The quality of your research determines the quality of your relevance,
and the quality of your relevance determines the quality of your
replies.**

Most people believe great cold email writers are simply better writers.

I disagree.

I believe they're better observers.

They notice things other people ignore.

They ask better questions.

They understand the prospect before trying to be understood.

### Stop Looking for Personalization. Start Looking for Relevance.

One of the biggest myths in cold outreach is that every email needs
heavy personalization.

You'll hear people say things like,

"You should mention their dog."

"Comment on their holiday picture."

"Reference their latest LinkedIn post."

Now let me ask you something.

If I started my email by saying,

"Congratulations on running the Lagos City Marathon."

...and then immediately started pitching my web design services...

Did mentioning the marathon actually improve my email?

No.

It had nothing to do with why I was reaching out.

That wasn't personalization.

That was decoration.

Unfortunately, a lot of people confuse the two.

Real personalization should make your email **more relevant**, not
simply prove that you spent five minutes stalking someone's LinkedIn
profile.

Never personalize just to impress someone.

Personalize only when it helps explain why you're reaching out.

### What Is Relevance?

Let's define it properly.

Relevance is the answer to one simple question.

**"Why should this person care about this email today?"**

Not tomorrow.

Not next year.

Today.

Every sentence you write should move the reader closer to answering that
question.

If your email isn't relevant, it doesn't matter how persuasive your
writing is.

People ignore irrelevant emails because their brains are designed to
ignore information that doesn't help them achieve their current goals.

That's human psychology.

### The Three Levels of Relevance

Now here's the good news.

You don't always need deep personalization to create relevance.

There are three levels of relevance, and understanding them will save
you hours of unnecessary research.

#### Level One: Personal Relevance

This is the strongest form of relevance because it's unique to the
individual.

You're referencing something directly connected to them.

Maybe they recently posted on LinkedIn.

Maybe they appeared on a podcast.

Maybe they announced a new product.

Maybe they spoke at an event.

Maybe they were promoted.

Those are all opportunities to create a natural opening.

For example:

Hi Sarah,

I listened to your interview on the Growth Marketing Podcast where you
mentioned expanding into East Africa.

That immediately tells Sarah this isn't a mass email.

But here's something important.

Don't stop there.

The observation should connect naturally to your reason for reaching
out.

If you say,

"I loved your podcast."

...and then suddenly pitch accounting software...

You've broken the connection.

Everything in your email should flow logically.

Observation.

Reason.

Conversation.

#### Level Two: Company Relevance

Sometimes you'll research someone and discover there's very little
information about them personally.

That's completely normal.

Instead of focusing on the individual, shift your attention to the
business.

Ask yourself:

What's happening inside this company right now?

Visit their website.

Read their blog.

Check their careers page.

Look at recent announcements.

Have they launched something?

Are they hiring?

Did they raise funding?

Did they open a new office?

Have they changed their pricing?

Every one of those tells a story.

Let's imagine you notice they're hiring ten sales representatives.

Instead of saying,

"I help companies increase sales."

You could say,

"I noticed your team is expanding its sales department. Congratulations.
Whenever companies begin growing their outbound teams, consistency in
messaging usually becomes much more important, which is one of the
reasons I thought I'd reach out."

Notice something.

We didn't assume they have a problem.

We simply connected what we observed to why we decided to email them.

That's relevance.

#### Level Three: Industry Relevance

This is the level most people forget exists.

Let's imagine you're emailing restaurants.

You don't know the owner.

The owner rarely posts on LinkedIn.

The company hasn't announced anything recently.

Does that mean you can't send a good email?

Of course not.

You still understand the industry.

You know restaurants often think about:

Customer retention.

Reservations.

Online reviews.

Repeat customers.

Staff shortages.

Food delivery.

Those aren't assumptions about one restaurant.

They're observations about the industry.

Your email might sound like this.

We've been speaking with several restaurant owners recently, and one
topic that keeps coming up is encouraging more repeat visits without
relying entirely on discounts. I wasn't sure if that's something your
team is currently thinking about, but I thought it was worth reaching
out.

See the difference?

We're discussing a pattern.

Not diagnosing their business.

### The Relevance Pyramid

Whenever you're researching a prospect, always search in this order.

First, look for something personal.

If you can't find anything meaningful, move to the company.

If there's nothing useful there, move to the industry.

Never force personalization.

A weak piece of personalization is often worse than strong industry
relevance.

### Where Should You Research?

Let's make this practical.

If I gave you ten minutes to research a prospect, where would you go?

Most people immediately say LinkedIn.

LinkedIn is useful, but it shouldn't be your only source.

Here's my research checklist.

Start with LinkedIn.

Look at their headline.

Read their About section.

Check their recent posts.

Look at what they comment on.

Then move to the company website.

Read the homepage.

Visit the About page.

Look at the product pages.

Read the blog if they have one.

Check the careers page.

A careers page tells you a lot about where a business is heading.

If they're hiring customer support staff, they may be growing rapidly.

If they're hiring salespeople, outbound may be a priority.

If they're hiring engineers, they're probably investing heavily in
product development.

Next, search for interviews.

Many founders appear on podcasts or webinars.

Those conversations often reveal priorities that never appear on their
website.

After that, look for industry news.

Has the industry changed recently?

Are there new regulations?

New competitors?

Emerging trends?

Remember, relevance doesn't always come from the individual.

Sometimes it comes from the environment they're operating in.

### Using Clay and AI the Right Way

Let's address something modern.

Many outbound teams use tools like Clay and AI to research prospects.

There's nothing wrong with that.

In fact, they can save a tremendous amount of time.

But tools don't replace thinking.

They accelerate it.

I've seen AI pull information like,

"Happy Birthday."

"Congratulations on your anniversary."

"Nice family vacation."

Just because AI found it doesn't mean it belongs in your email.

Before including any piece of information, ask yourself one question.

**Does this make my email more relevant to why I'm reaching out?**

If the answer is no, remove it.

The best researchers aren't the people who collect the most information.

They're the people who know what information matters.

### Qualifying Your Prospect Before You Email

Here's another mistake beginners make.

They think everyone is a prospect.

They're not.

Before writing a single email, ask yourself these questions.

Can this person actually make the decision I'm hoping they'll make?

Does my solution fit their business?

Is there a genuine reason for us to have a conversation?

Can I explain why I'm contacting them in one sentence?

If you can't answer those questions confidently, you're probably
emailing the wrong person.

The easiest way to improve your reply rate isn't always to write better
emails.

Sometimes it's simply to email better prospects.

### Key Takeaways

Before we move to the next chapter, remember these principles.

Research is not a separate step from writing. It is the foundation of
good writing.

Don't confuse personalization with relevance.

Use the Relevance Pyramid: Personal, Company, Industry.

Never make unsupported assumptions about someone's business.

Research to understand, not to impress.

The better you understand your prospect's world, the easier it becomes
to write an email that feels like it belongs in their inbox rather than
interrupting it.

# Chapter 3 — The Anatomy of a Cold Email

*"Most people think there's a secret template that gets replies. There
isn't. Templates don't get replies. Good communication does. Once you
understand the anatomy of a great cold email, you can write one for any
industry, any service and almost any audience."*

Now that we've covered mindset, psychology, research and relevance, it's
finally time to write.

Before we do, I want you to forget about templates for a moment.

Templates are useful, but they can also become a crutch.

I've seen people download a template from the internet, replace a few
words with their prospect's name and company, then wonder why nobody
replies.

The reason is simple.

They copied the words without understanding the structure.

Think about building a house.

If you know where the foundation goes, where the walls go and where the
roof goes, you can build different types of houses.

The same principle applies to cold emails.

Once you understand the structure, you don't need to memorize templates.

You can build your own.

Today, we're going to break a cold email into five parts.

Every successful cold email, whether it's written by a freelancer, an
agency, a recruiter or a salesperson, follows these same principles.

Those five parts are:

The Opening

The Bridge

Credibility

The Call to Action

The Close

Every sentence you write should have a purpose.

If it doesn't, remove it.

## Part One

### The Opening

The opening decides whether the rest of your email gets read.

If the opening feels generic, the reader will assume the rest of the
email is generic too.

This is why your first sentence carries so much weight.

Now let me ask you something.

How many emails begin like this?

Hi, my name is David.

Or

I hope you're having a wonderful day.

Or

My company specializes in...

None of those sentences give the reader a reason to continue.

They delay the interesting part.

Your opening should answer one question as quickly as possible.

**"Why am I receiving this email?"**

That's why I always recommend starting with an observation.

Not an opinion.

Not an assumption.

An observation.

For example,

I noticed your company recently expanded into Kenya.

Or,

I came across your interview on the SaaS Growth Podcast.

Or,

I saw that your team is currently hiring five Account Executives.

Notice something.

Every one of those statements is true.

We're not exaggerating.

We're not diagnosing.

We're simply demonstrating that this email has context.

That's important because context creates credibility.

### What Makes a Good Observation?

A good observation does three things.

First, it's true.

Never invent information.

Second, it's relevant to why you're reaching out.

Third, it naturally leads into the next sentence.

Let's compare two openings.

#### Example One

I noticed you enjoy golfing.

Imagine you're trying to sell HR software.

Does golfing have anything to do with HR software?

Probably not.

Now look at this.

I noticed your team has doubled in size over the past six months.

Now your email has direction.

The observation connects to the conversation you're about to start.

Always ask yourself,

**"If I remove this observation, does my email lose context?"**

If the answer is no, it probably wasn't worth including.

## Part Two

### The Bridge

This is the part almost nobody teaches.

Most people jump from the observation straight into a sales pitch.

Instead, you need a bridge.

The bridge explains why your observation led you to send the email.

Think of it as connecting two dots.

Observation.

Then explanation.

For example,

I noticed your company has been hiring aggressively over the past few
months.

Now the bridge.

That made me wonder how you're keeping messaging consistent across a
growing sales team.

Notice the language.

We didn't say,

"You probably have inconsistent messaging."

We didn't say,

"This is costing you revenue."

We simply explained what we were wondering.

The bridge feels natural because it reflects how people think.

"I saw this."

"So it made me wonder about that."

That's a conversation.

Not a pitch.

### Avoid Diagnosing Businesses

One of the quickest ways to lose credibility is pretending you know
what's happening inside someone else's company.

Let's compare two approaches.

#### Poor Example

Your website is costing you leads.

Do we know that?

No.

We haven't seen their analytics.

We haven't spoken to their customers.

We're guessing.

Now compare that to this.

I may be wrong, but I wondered whether improving the enquiry process has
been a priority recently.

Which feels more respectful?

The second one.

Because it leaves room for the prospect to confirm or correct you.

That's exactly what conversations are supposed to do.

One of my favourite phrases is,

"I may be wrong..."

Why?

Because it lowers resistance.

It tells the reader,

"I'm not pretending to know your business better than you do."

And people appreciate that honesty.

Other useful phrases include,

"I wasn't sure whether..."

"From the outside looking in..."

"I wondered if..."

"There's a chance you've already solved this..."

These phrases don't weaken your email.

They make it believable.

## Part Three

### Credibility

Now that you've established relevance, it's time to answer another
question.

**"Why should I listen to you?"**

This is where credibility comes in.

Notice I didn't say bragging.

There's a difference.

Many people think credibility means writing something like,

"We're the leading agency in Africa."

Or,

"We're the number one experts."

According to who?

Those are opinions.

Not evidence.

Real credibility comes from proof.

For example,

We've helped three logistics companies automate their customer updates.

Or,

We recently worked with a growing fintech company facing a similar
challenge.

Or,

We've seen this approach reduce manual work for several teams we've
supported.

Notice how none of those examples sound arrogant.

They're calm.

Specific.

Believable.

That's what credibility should feel like.

### How Much Credibility Should You Include?

Here's a mistake I see all the time.

People spend six paragraphs talking about themselves.

Their awards.

Their experience.

Their certifications.

Their team.

Their office.

Their clients.

Remember Chapter One?

Your email isn't about you.

One sentence is usually enough.

If someone wants to know more, they'll ask.

Or they'll visit your website.

Your job isn't to answer every question.

Your job is to earn the next conversation.

## Part Four

### The Call to Action

This is where many cold emails fall apart.

People spend time researching.

They write a strong opening.

They establish relevance.

Then they ruin everything with a poor CTA.

For example,

Are you available for a forty-five minute demo next Tuesday?

Think about that.

You're asking a complete stranger for forty-five minutes of their time.

That's a huge commitment.

Remember what we discussed in Chapter One.

People are much more willing to make small commitments than large ones.

So instead of asking for a meeting immediately, ask yourself,

**"What's the smallest next step?"**

Maybe it's simply getting a reply.

Maybe it's permission to send a resource.

Maybe it's finding out whether this is even relevant.

Here are a few examples.

Is this something you've been thinking about recently?

Happy to send over a short guide if it would be useful.

Worth exploring further?

Curious whether this is currently on your radar.

Notice how conversational these feel.

You're inviting a discussion.

Not forcing a decision.

### Match Your CTA to Your Goal

This is something most people overlook.

Your CTA should depend on what you're trying to achieve.

If your goal is to share a resource, ask for permission.

If your goal is to validate an idea, ask a question.

If your goal is to book a meeting, make sure you've earned that request.

Don't ask for more than you've earned.

## Part Five

### The Close

Believe it or not, people overthink this part too.

Some write paragraphs thanking the prospect.

Others include motivational quotes.

Some add long email signatures with five phone numbers and ten social
media links.

Keep it simple.

A professional sign-off is enough.

For example,

Best,

David

Or,

Kind regards,

Sarah

Your signature isn't what gets replies.

Your email does.

## Putting It All Together

Let's see how these pieces work as one conversation.

Observation.

I noticed your company recently expanded your customer support team.

Bridge.

It made me wonder how you're maintaining consistency as new team members
come onboard.

Credibility.

We've worked with several growing SaaS companies to simplify onboarding
and internal knowledge sharing.

CTA.

Is this something you've already solved, or is it something you're
currently exploring?

That's it.

No hype.

No exaggerated claims.

No unnecessary paragraphs.

Just a conversation.

## The Three Sentence Test

Before sending any cold email, read it one sentence at a time and ask
yourself these questions.

Sentence One.

Does this immediately establish relevance?

Sentence Two.

Does this naturally explain why I'm reaching out?

Sentence Three.

Does this build credibility without sounding arrogant?

Sentence Four.

Does this make the next step feel easy?

If every sentence has a clear purpose, you're on the right track.

If you find a sentence that exists only to make you sound impressive,
delete it.

Remember, clarity beats cleverness every single time.

Did the credibility feel believable?

Did the CTA feel easy to respond to?

If they hesitate on any answer, revise it.

Because before you worry about fancy copywriting techniques, you need to
master the anatomy.

Strong cold emails aren't built with clever words.

They're built with clear thinking, genuine relevance and purposeful
structure.

# Chapter 4 — The Three Core Cold Email Structures

*"If you understand these three structures, you'll never have to ask
anyone for a cold email template again."*

Now that we've learned the anatomy of a cold email, let's answer a
question I get almost every week.

"Can you send me a cold email template?"

I always smile when I hear that because it tells me exactly where the
person is in their outbound journey.

Beginners collect templates.

Professionals understand frameworks.

Let me explain the difference.

A template is a finished email.

A framework is the thinking behind that email.

Think about a chef.

A beginner follows a recipe word for word.

An experienced chef understands flavours, ingredients and techniques.
Give them different ingredients and they can still make an excellent
meal.

Cold emailing works the same way.

Once you understand the frameworks, you don't need to copy someone
else's email.

You can write your own.

Today I'm going to teach you the three structures I've found to be the
most versatile across different industries and offers.

Can you create other structures?

Absolutely.

But if you master these three, you'll be able to write effective cold
emails for almost any situation.

## Structure One

### Observation, Assumption, Solution, Question

This is probably the easiest structure to learn and one of the most
effective for agencies, freelancers, consultants and service providers.

It feels natural because it mirrors how conversations happen in real
life.

Let's break it down.

#### Step One: Observation

Start with something you've genuinely observed.

Remember what we covered in the last chapter.

An observation should be true.

Relevant.

And connected to why you're reaching out.

For example,

"I noticed your team recently launched a new customer portal."

Or,

"I saw your company is expanding into Ghana."

Or,

"I came across your webinar on customer retention."

At this stage, you're simply setting the context.

Nothing more.

#### Step Two: Assumption

This is where many people go wrong.

They confuse assumptions with accusations.

Let me show you the difference.

Bad example.

Your onboarding process is probably broken.

That's an accusation disguised as an assumption.

Now look at this.

I may be wrong, but I wondered whether onboarding has become a bigger
focus as your customer base grows.

Notice the difference?

We're inviting the prospect into a conversation.

Not telling them what their business looks like.

Your assumptions should always leave room for the prospect to say,

"Actually, we've already solved that."

Or,

"You're right. That's something we're working on."

Either response creates a conversation.

And that's the goal.

#### Step Three: Solution

Now explain why you decided to reach out.

Keep it short.

Don't tell your company's life story.

Instead, connect your expertise to the assumption you just made.

For example,

"We've been helping growing SaaS companies simplify customer onboarding
through interactive product tours."

Notice that we're not listing every service we offer.

We're only talking about the one that's relevant.

One email.

One problem.

One solution.

#### Step Four: Question

End with a simple question.

Examples include,

"Is this something you're currently exploring?"

"Has this been on your radar recently?"

"Would it make sense to compare notes?"

Avoid asking for too much too soon.

Remember Chapter One.

The goal is to earn a reply.

Not a contract.

#### Full Example

Hi Sarah,

I noticed your company recently expanded your customer success team.

I may be wrong, but I wondered whether maintaining a consistent
onboarding experience has become more important as the team grows.

We've been helping several SaaS companies build onboarding systems that
reduce ramp-up time for new customers.

Is this something you've been thinking about recently?

Best,

David

Simple.

Conversational.

Relevant.

### When Should You Use This Structure?

Use this structure when:

You have done meaningful research.

You have a clear observation.

Your solution naturally connects to that observation.

This is my default framework because it feels the least like a sales
email and the most like a genuine conversation.

## Structure Two

### Industry Pattern, Proof, Curiosity

Sometimes you can't find anything meaningful about the individual.

Maybe they don't post online.

Maybe the company rarely shares updates.

Should you give up?

Not at all.

This is where industry knowledge becomes your advantage.

Instead of talking about one business, you talk about a pattern you've
observed across similar businesses.

Notice the wording.

A pattern.

Not a diagnosis.

#### Step One: Industry Pattern

For example,

"We've been speaking with several accounting firms recently."

Or,

"One conversation we've been having with manufacturers..."

Or,

"Across the logistics companies we've worked with..."

This immediately tells the reader,

"I'm not making assumptions about you specifically."

"I'm sharing something we've consistently observed."

That feels much more credible.

#### Step Two: Explain the Pattern

Now describe the recurring challenge.

For example,

"A recurring theme has been reducing manual reporting without adding
more software."

Or,

"One topic that keeps coming up is improving customer retention without
increasing advertising spend."

Again, you're describing a pattern.

Not diagnosing.

#### Step Three: Proof

Now explain what happened.

For example,

"We recently helped one client reduce reporting time by six hours each
week."

Or,

"We've seen several companies improve response times after introducing
this process."

Notice something.

Proof doesn't need to be dramatic.

It just needs to be believable.

#### Step Four: Curiosity

Instead of immediately asking for a meeting, invite curiosity.

For example,

"I wasn't sure whether that's something your team has been thinking
about as well."

Or,

"Curious whether this has come up internally."

Curiosity opens conversations.

Pressure closes them.

#### Full Example

Hi James,

We've been speaking with several logistics companies over the past few
months.

One topic that keeps coming up is keeping customers updated without
creating extra work for support teams.

We've helped several companies automate those updates while improving
customer satisfaction.

I wasn't sure whether that's something your team is exploring as well,
but I thought it was worth reaching out.

Best,

David

### When Should You Use This Structure?

Use this framework when:

You understand the industry well.

You have experience solving similar problems.

You don't have strong personal or company-specific research.

This is one of the safest structures because you're discussing trends
rather than pretending to know someone's business.

## Structure Three

### Compliment, Insight, Invitation

This structure is especially effective when emailing founders,
executives, creators and thought leaders.

These people often receive hundreds of emails every week.

Many of those emails begin with a sales pitch.

We're going to do something different.

We're going to begin with genuine appreciation.

Notice I said genuine.

Not flattery.

There's a difference.

#### Compliment

A compliment should recognise something meaningful.

Not something obvious.

Don't say,

"I love your company."

That's lazy.

Instead,

"I enjoyed your recent article about hiring remote teams."

Or,

"I watched your keynote on customer experience."

Be specific.

#### Insight

Now explain what stood out to you.

This is where you demonstrate that you actually paid attention.

For example,

"You mentioned that speed often matters more than perfection during the
early stages of growth."

Or,

"One thing you said about customer education really made me think."

You're not selling.

You're engaging.

#### Invitation

Now invite them into a conversation.

Examples include,

"It made me curious whether you'd be open to hearing another perspective
we've observed."

Or,

"I'd love to hear your thoughts on something similar we've seen while
working with other founders."

Notice how collaborative this feels.

You're not positioning yourself as someone trying to sell.

You're positioning yourself as someone adding to the discussion.

#### Full Example

Hi Michael,

I really enjoyed your article about building customer loyalty.

One point that stood out was your belief that customer education is one
of the biggest drivers of retention.

That reminded me of something we've consistently observed while helping
SaaS companies improve product adoption.

I'd be interested to know whether you've seen something similar.

Best,

David

This structure works because people enjoy talking about ideas they've
already shown interest in.

## Which Structure Should You Use?

Students ask me this all the time.

The answer is simple.

It depends on your research.

If you've found a strong observation about the individual or the
company, use Structure One.

If your strength lies in your knowledge of the industry, use Structure
Two.

If you're reaching out to founders, executives or thought leaders about
something they've published or spoken about, Structure Three often works
best.

Don't force one structure to fit every situation.

Choose the framework that best matches the context.

## Common Mistakes Across All Three Structures

Now that you've learned the frameworks, let's look at the mistakes
people still make.

#### Mistake One

Trying to sound clever.

Good cold emails aren't memorable because they use fancy words.

They're memorable because they're easy to understand.

#### Mistake Two

Adding unnecessary information.

Every sentence should answer one question.

Does this move the conversation forward?

If not, remove it.

#### Mistake Three

Talking too much about yourself.

Remember Chapter One.

Your prospect cares about themselves first.

Talk about yourself only when it helps build credibility.

#### Mistake Four

Making unsupported claims.

Don't tell someone they're losing customers.

Don't tell them they're wasting money.

Don't tell them their process is broken.

Unless you know those things to be true, don't say them.

Replace certainty with curiosity.

"I wondered whether..."

"I wasn't sure if..."

"I may be wrong..."

These phrases invite dialogue instead of creating resistance.

#### Mistake Five

Using the same framework for everyone.

Context matters.

A founder is different from a recruiter.

A dentist is different from a software company.

A local business is different from an enterprise organisation.

Adapt your framework accordingly.

Now write three different emails.

framework.

The second should use the Industry Pattern, Proof, Curiosity framework.

The third should use the Compliment, Insight, Invitation framework.

Don't worry about making them perfect.

The goal is to understand how changing the structure changes the

Once you've finished, exchange emails with a partner.

Discuss which structure feels the most natural for that prospect and,
more importantly, why.

You'll quickly realise there isn't one perfect cold email.

There is only the framework that's most appropriate for the person
you're writing to.

# Chapter 5 — High Converting Cold Email Templates and How to Adapt Them

*"One of the biggest mistakes people make is treating templates as
answers. They're not. Templates are examples. The moment you copy them
word for word, they stop working."*

If there's one thing people ask me for more than anything else, it's
this.

"Can you send me a cold email template?"

I understand why.

Templates feel safe.

You look at one and think, "If I just copy this, I'll get the same
results."

Unfortunately, that's not how cold email works.

Let me ask you a question.

If I gave everyone in this room the exact same email template and asked
you all to email the same CEO, what do you think would happen?

Some people would get replies.

Some wouldn't.

Why?

Because a template isn't what gets replies.

The thinking behind the template does.

A template is simply a practical example of a framework.

Think of it like learning to drive.

Watching someone else drive doesn't automatically make you a good
driver.

You need to understand why they're braking, why they're changing gears
and why they're taking a particular route.

The same applies here.

Today, I'm going to give you several templates.

But more importantly, I'm going to teach you how to think about them so
you can adapt them to your own business, industry and prospects.

Never become dependent on templates.

Become dependent on principles.

## Before You Choose a Template

Don't ask,

"Which template converts the best?"

Ask,

**"Which template fits this situation?"**

That's a much better question.

The right email for a founder isn't always the right email for a
recruiter.

The right email for a local restaurant isn't always the right email for
a SaaS company.

Your template should fit three things.

Who you're emailing.

What you're offering.

What you want them to do next.

When those three things align, the email feels natural.

When they don't, it feels forced.

## Template One

### The Curious Observer

This is one of the safest and most versatile templates you'll ever use.

It's ideal for freelancers, agencies and consultants.

It follows the structure we learned in the previous chapter.

Observation.

Assumption.

Relevance.

Question.

Here's what it looks like.

Hi Sarah,

I noticed your company recently expanded your customer support team.

I may be wrong, but I wondered whether maintaining a consistent
onboarding experience has become a bigger focus as the team grows.

We've been helping several SaaS companies simplify onboarding through
interactive product guides.

Is this something you've been exploring recently?

Let's break down why this works.

The observation creates context.

The assumption invites conversation instead of making accusations.

The relevance explains why you're reaching out.

The question makes it easy to reply.

Notice what isn't here.

No long introduction.

No company history.

No list of services.

No pressure.

### When Should You Use It?

Use this template when you've found a meaningful observation during your
research.

If you haven't done your research, don't use this template.

It won't feel authentic.

## Template Two

### The Industry Insight

Sometimes you can't find enough information about the individual.

That's perfectly okay.

Use industry relevance instead.

Here's an example.

Hi James,

We've been speaking with several logistics companies recently, and one
topic that keeps coming up is keeping customers informed without
creating extra work for support teams.

We've helped businesses in similar situations automate those updates
while improving customer satisfaction.

I wasn't sure whether this is something your team has been discussing as
well, but I thought it was worth reaching out.

Let's break it down.

The first sentence establishes familiarity with the industry.

The second sentence provides credibility.

The final sentence creates curiosity without assuming anything.

This template works because it says,

"We understand your world."

Not,

"We know your business."

There's a big difference.

## Template Three

### The Resource First Approach

One of the biggest mistakes in outbound is asking for something before
you've offered anything.

Imagine walking up to a stranger and saying,

"Can I borrow fifty dollars?"

It feels uncomfortable because nothing has been exchanged.

Now imagine saying,

"I noticed something that might help you. Would you like me to share
it?"

Very different.

This is where lead magnets become powerful.

Here's an example.

Hi Sarah,

I recently put together a short checklist on reducing onboarding
friction for SaaS teams.

Since your company has been growing quickly, I thought it might be
relevant.

Happy to send it over if you'd find it useful.

Notice something.

We're not asking for a meeting.

We're offering value first.

Sometimes the easiest way to earn a conversation is to stop asking for
one immediately.

### What Makes a Good Lead Magnet?

A good lead magnet solves one small problem.

It doesn't try to teach everything.

Examples include:

A two-minute Loom video.

A short audit.

A benchmark report.

A checklist.

A playbook.

A framework.

A case study.

A calculator.

A swipe file.

A one-page guide.

Keep it practical.

People are far more likely to read a two-page checklist than a
ninety-page ebook.

## Template Four

### The Referral Template

Sometimes the person you're emailing isn't the right decision maker.

That's okay.

Instead of trying to force the conversation, ask for direction.

For example,

Hi David,

I wasn't sure if you're the right person to speak with regarding
customer onboarding.

If not, would you mind pointing me towards whoever owns that process?

Simple.

Respectful.

People are often willing to help when you're asking for guidance rather
than making demands.

## Template Five

### The Partnership Template

Not every cold email is trying to sell something.

Sometimes you're looking for collaborations.

Joint ventures.

Podcast appearances.

Strategic partnerships.

Those emails should sound different.

For example,

Hi Sarah,

I've been following the work your team has been doing around financial
literacy, and I noticed we're speaking to very similar audiences.

It made me wonder whether there might be an opportunity for us to
collaborate on a webinar or resource that benefits both communities.

If that's something you'd be open to exploring, I'd love to exchange a
few ideas.

Notice the tone.

It's collaborative.

Not transactional.

## Template Six

### The Job Opportunity Template

Cold emailing isn't just for sales.

It's one of the most effective job search tools available.

Instead of waiting for job advertisements, you create opportunities.

For example,

Hi Michael,

I came across your company while researching businesses doing
interesting work in cybersecurity.

I was particularly interested in your recent expansion into managed
security services.

I've spent the last three years helping organisations strengthen their
cloud security, and I wondered whether you'd be open to a short
conversation if opportunities arise in the future.

Even if they're not hiring today, you've started a relationship.

## How to Adapt Any Template

This is the part most courses never teach.

They give you templates.

They don't teach adaptation.

Every template can be customised by changing five things.

The observation.

The assumption.

The proof.

The offer.

The CTA.

Everything else stays relatively similar.

Let's imagine you're a web designer.

Your proof might change.

Your observation might change.

Your CTA might change.

The overall structure doesn't.

That's why understanding frameworks matters more than memorising words.

## Choosing the Right CTA

Earlier in the course we talked about reducing commitment.

Let's build on that.

Not every email should end with,

"Can we book a meeting?"

Sometimes another CTA makes much more sense.

If you're offering a guide,

Ask permission to send it.

If you've recorded a Loom,

Ask whether they'd like to see it.

If you've completed a small audit,

Offer to share it.

If you're validating an idea,

Ask for their opinion.

If you've already built enough trust,

Then ask for a meeting.

The CTA should match the relationship.

Don't ask for a marathon when you've only introduced yourself.

## Why Lead Magnets Increase Reply Rates

Let's spend a little more time on lead magnets because they're one of
the easiest ways to improve your outreach.

Most cold emails ask for time.

Time is expensive.

A lead magnet gives value before asking for time.

Think about it.

Which feels easier?

"Can we schedule thirty minutes?"

Or,

"I recorded a two-minute video with a couple of observations. Happy to
send it over if you're interested."

The second feels much safer.

There's almost no risk.

That's exactly what you want.

Reduce friction.

Increase curiosity.

## Common Mistakes When Using Templates

The first mistake is copying them word for word.

Templates become less effective the more people use them.

Treat them as inspiration.

Not scripts.

The second mistake is over-personalising.

Remember Chapter Two.

Not every personal detail belongs in your email.

If it doesn't strengthen relevance, remove it.

The third mistake is making your offer too broad.

Don't tell someone everything your business does.

Tell them the one thing that's relevant to them.

The fourth mistake is forgetting to sound human.

Read your email out loud.

Would you actually say those words in a conversation?

If not, rewrite them.

Cold emails should sound like one professional talking to another.

Not like a marketing brochure.

Choose one prospect.

Now write three different emails.

The first should use the Curious Observer template.

The third should use the Resource First template.

Now compare them.

Ask yourself:

Which one feels the most relevant?

Which one feels the most natural?

Which one would you be most likely to reply to if you were the prospect?

Great cold email writers don't memorise templates.

And that's what separates someone who sends emails from someone who

### Chapter Summary

Templates don't get replies. Principles do.

Choose a template based on the situation, not because it's popular.

Always adapt your template to the prospect, the offer and the goal.

Offer value before asking for commitment whenever possible.

Use lead magnets to reduce friction and increase curiosity.

Most importantly, write like a human being. The best cold emails don't
sound like marketing. They sound like the beginning of a worthwhile
conversation.

# Chapter 6 — Subject Lines, Lead Magnets, Follow Ups and Deliverability

*"You can write the best cold email in the world, but if nobody opens
it, nobody reads it. If nobody reads it, nobody replies. This chapter is
about everything that happens before and after your email is read."*

Up until this point, we've focused on writing.

Now we're going to focus on what surrounds the writing.

Think of cold emailing like opening a shop.

Your offer may be fantastic, but if nobody walks through the front door,
you'll never make a sale.

Your subject line is the front door.

Your follow up keeps the conversation alive.

Your deliverability determines whether anyone even sees your email.

Let's start with the first thing your prospect sees.

## Subject Lines

One of the biggest misconceptions in cold outreach is that subject lines
need to be clever.

They don't.

In fact, the more clever you try to be, the more likely you are to sound
like marketing.

Remember something.

Your goal isn't to trick someone into opening your email.

Your goal is to give them a reason to.

There is a big difference.

A misleading subject line may get an open.

It probably won't get a reply.

Trust begins before your email is opened.

It begins with your subject line.

### What Makes Someone Open an Email?

Ask yourself this question.

When you're checking your own inbox, why do you choose one email over
another?

Usually one of four reasons.

You recognise the sender.

The subject feels relevant.

You're curious.

Or the email looks important.

As a stranger, you don't have the advantage of familiarity.

That means you need relevance and curiosity.

Not clickbait.

Genuine curiosity.

### The Best Subject Lines Feel Normal

Read these two examples.

**Example One**

Increase Your Revenue by 300 Percent in 30 Days!!!

**Example Two**

Which one feels like it came from a real person?

The second.

Your subject line should look like something a colleague might send.

Not something a marketer spent two hours optimising.

### Subject Line Categories

Let's look at a few categories that consistently work well.

#### Curiosity

This you?

Curious about something

Thought I'd ask

One observation

#### Context

Regarding your hiring

About your recent webinar

Question about your sales team

Following your announcement

#### Resource Based

A short guide

Recorded something for you

Customer onboarding checklist

Thought this might help

#### Referral

Not sure who owns this

Could you point me in the right direction?

### What Should You Avoid?

Avoid words that immediately make your email look promotional.

Free

Guaranteed

Limited offer

Act now

Exclusive

Amazing

Best ever

Revolutionary

You don't lose replies because your subject line is too simple.

You lose replies because your subject line feels like an advertisement.

## Lead Magnets

Earlier in the course, we introduced lead magnets.

Now let's understand why they work so well.

Most cold emails ask for time.

Meetings.

Calls.

Demonstrations.

Time is one of the most valuable things you can ask another person to
give you.

Especially when you're a stranger.

A lead magnet changes that dynamic.

Instead of asking for value first...

You provide value first.

That's a much easier conversation to start.

### What Makes a Great Lead Magnet?

Many businesses think a lead magnet has to be a huge ebook.

It doesn't.

In fact, shorter usually performs better.

The best lead magnets solve one specific problem.

Not ten.

Here are some examples.

A two minute Loom video reviewing their website.

A personalised audit.

An industry benchmark report.

A pricing comparison.

A one page checklist.

An email teardown.

A playbook.

A calculator.

A swipe file.

A framework.

Notice something.

Almost every one of these can be consumed in less than ten minutes.

That's intentional.

People are busy.

Respect their time.

### Match the Lead Magnet to the Prospect

A mistake many businesses make is sending everyone the same resource.

Imagine sending a guide about LinkedIn prospecting to a manufacturing
company.

It's probably not relevant.

Instead, think about the prospect's role.

If you're emailing a Head of Sales...

Share something related to pipeline generation.

If you're emailing HR...

Share something related to hiring or onboarding.

If you're emailing a founder...

Share something related to growth, efficiency or strategy.

The more relevant the resource, the more valuable it feels.

### Don't Hide Your Best Ideas

Some people worry that if they give away too much information, nobody
will hire them.

I disagree.

Giving away useful ideas builds trust.

People don't hire experts because they know nothing.

They hire experts because they don't have the time, experience or
resources to execute everything themselves.

Don't be afraid to demonstrate your expertise.

Just don't overwhelm the reader.

## Follow Ups

Let's have an honest conversation.

How many follow ups should you send?

There isn't one perfect number.

But here's something I've observed over the years.

Many opportunities are lost because people stop too early.

At the same time...

Many opportunities are also lost because people become annoying.

The goal isn't persistence at all costs.

The goal is professional persistence.

There is a difference.

### Why Follow Ups Work

Imagine someone receives your email on Monday morning.

They're in meetings all day.

Tuesday they're travelling.

Wednesday they finally return to their inbox.

Did they ignore your email?

Maybe.

Or maybe life simply got in the way.

Not every non response is a rejection.

Sometimes it's just poor timing.

That's why respectful follow ups matter.

### A Simple Four Email Sequence

#### Email One

Introduce yourself.

Create relevance.

Start a conversation.

Nothing more.

#### Email Two

Three or four days later.

Keep it short.

For example.

Hi Sarah,

Just wanted to bring this back to the top of your inbox in case it got
buried.

Curious whether this is something you've been thinking about recently.

Notice that we're not adding pressure.

We're simply reminding.

#### Email Three

Instead of repeating yourself...

Add value.

Share a resource.

Mention an insight.

Offer a short video.

Send a checklist.

Teach them something.

Every follow up should either create new value or new context.

Never just say,

"Following up."

Again and again.

#### Email Four

The Break Up Email

This is one of the most misunderstood emails in outbound.

A break up email isn't designed to make someone feel guilty.

It's designed to remove pressure.

Here's an example.

Hi Sarah,

I haven't heard back, so I'll assume the timing isn't right.

No worries at all.

If this becomes relevant in the future, feel free to reach out.

Wishing you and the team all the best.

Simple.

Respectful.

Professional.

Ironically, these emails often receive replies because people no longer
feel they're being chased.

## Knowing When to Stop

One of the hardest skills in sales is knowing when enough is enough.

If someone clearly says they're not interested...

Respect that.

Don't argue.

Don't try to convince them.

Thank them for their honesty.

Leave the door open.

People remember professionals who respect boundaries.

## Deliverability

Let's move away from writing for a moment.

Imagine you've written the perfect cold email.

Nobody replies.

Later you discover it never reached the inbox.

Everything we've learned becomes useless if your emails don't get
delivered.

Deliverability isn't exciting.

But it's essential.

### Why Emails Go to Spam

Email providers want to protect users.

If your behaviour looks suspicious, they'll filter your messages.

Some common reasons include:

Sending too many emails from a brand new domain.

Missing authentication records.

Large numbers of bounced emails.

Too many spam complaints.

Poor engagement.

Sending identical emails to hundreds of people.

Deliverability isn't about beating spam filters.

It's about behaving like a trustworthy sender.

### Understand the Basics

You don't need to become a technical expert.

But you should understand a few terms.

SPF helps verify that your email server is authorised to send messages.

DKIM adds a digital signature that proves your email hasn't been
altered.

DMARC tells receiving email providers how to handle messages that fail
authentication.

Think of these as your email's identification documents.

Without them, it's much harder to build trust with inbox providers.

### Warm Up Your Domain

Imagine creating a brand new email address today.

Tomorrow you send one thousand cold emails.

What do you think Gmail will assume?

Probably that you're a spammer.

Instead, build trust gradually.

Start with small numbers.

Send genuine conversations.

Increase volume over time.

Trust isn't built overnight.

Neither is deliverability.

### Sending Limits

Quality always beats quantity.

I'd rather see someone send fifty thoughtful emails than five hundred
irrelevant ones.

Scaling poor outreach doesn't create better results.

It creates bigger problems.

Scale only after you've proven your messaging works.

## AI in Cold Outreach

Since AI has become part of modern outbound, let's talk about how to use
it properly.

AI is excellent for:

Research.

Generating ideas.

Improving grammar.

Summarising information.

Creating first drafts.

AI is terrible when you ask it to replace your thinking.

Don't tell AI,

"Write me a cold email."

Instead say,

"Help me improve this opening."

Or,

"Suggest three different CTAs."

Use AI as an assistant.

Not as your personality.

Remember something.

People buy from people.

Not robots.

## Common Mistakes

Let's finish this chapter with a few mistakes that can undo all your
hard work.

Writing misleading subject lines.

Offering generic lead magnets.

Following up without adding value.

Sending too many emails too quickly.

Ignoring deliverability.

Using AI without editing the output.

Asking for too much too soon.

And perhaps the biggest mistake of all...

Giving up after only one or two emails.

## Class Exercise

You're going to work in small groups.

Each group will receive a prospect profile.

Your task is to create:

Three subject lines.

One relevant lead magnet.

A four email follow up sequence.

Finally, explain why you made those choices.

Not just what you wrote.

Remember, in cold outreach every decision should have a reason behind
it.

The best outbound professionals don't guess.

They think deliberately.

### Chapter Summary

Your subject line earns the open.

Your email earns the reply.

Your follow ups create additional opportunities.

Your deliverability determines whether your message is even seen.

Treat all four with equal importance.

Because success in cold emailing isn't about writing one great email.

It's about building a complete outreach system where every step supports
the next.
